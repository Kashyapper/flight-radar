How Much Do Planes Really Pollute?

About 100,000 flights take off every day. Here is how much pollution they cause, how that compares with everything else people do, and what could bring it down.

November 2026

Every day, people around the world release about 146 million tonnes of greenhouse gases. Most of it is carbon dioxide (CO₂) from burning coal, oil and gas. The rest is mainly methane, which comes largely from farming and fossil fuels, plus nitrous oxide and industrial gases. Over a full year, that adds up to about 53 billion tonnes.

Planes are one piece of that total. All the world's flights together release about 2.7 million tonnes of CO₂ a day, roughly the same as all the world's cargo ships. That's about 2.5% of humanity's CO₂, or about 1.8% of all greenhouse gases. The table below puts every number in this article in one place.

Putting the big numbers side by side

Planes and ships pollute about equally, and each one releases nearly four times as much CO₂ as every volcano on Earth combined. Volcanoes are often blamed for climate change, but humans release as much CO₂ in under three days as all the world's volcanoes do in a year. Even the eruptions of Mount St. Helens in 1980 and Pinatubo in 1991 only matched human output for about nine hours.

On a smaller scale, an average flight releases about 26 tonnes of CO₂, the same as about 2,000 cars each driving for a day. That's for the whole plane, not one passenger, and it's an average: long flights release far more, short ones far less. A car releases about 12.6 kg a day, and a dairy cow releases about 10 kg of CO₂e a day, almost all of it methane from digestion.

You also breathe out about 1 kg of CO₂ a day, but it isn't counted as pollution. That carbon came from food, and the plants behind that food pulled it out of the air recently, so breathing just completes a natural loop. Burning fuel is different, because it releases carbon that had been locked underground for millions of years.

The part CO₂ numbers miss

Planes cause more warming than their CO₂ alone suggests. When they fly high, they leave contrails, the white lines across the sky. Some of these spread into thin clouds that trap heat. Jet engines also release nitrogen oxides, which add more warming. Planes release almost no methane or nitrous oxide, so this is where their extra impact comes from.

Counting all of these effects, scientists estimate that flying causes about 3.5% of human-caused warming. That's more than its 2.5% share of CO₂, but still a small slice of the whole. Power plants, factories and road traffic are all far bigger. The worry with flying is the trend: planes keep getting more efficient, but the number of flights keeps growing faster, so total emissions keep rising.

What can be done

Airlines have made real progress per trip. Between 2018 and 2025, the CO₂ released for every tonne of passengers and cargo carried one kilometre fell by about 20%, thanks to newer planes and better operations. Because there are so many more flights, total emissions still went up. Sustainable aviation fuel, made from waste, plants or captured carbon, is the industry's main hope, but it's growing slowly. In 2025, it cut about 1% of airline CO₂ when combined with carbon offsets. Since contrails cause so much of the warming, researchers are also testing whether small changes in altitude can help planes avoid the air where long-lasting contrails form.

For travellers, the biggest choice is simply how often to fly. For short trips, a train or bus usually releases much less per person. When flying does make sense, a nonstop flight avoids a second takeoff and climb, the most fuel-hungry part of any trip, and economy seats spread each flight's emissions across more people. None of this will fix the problem alone, but flying is one of the few areas where one person's choices make a noticeable difference to their own footprint.
