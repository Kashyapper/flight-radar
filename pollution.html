How Much Do Planes Really Pollute?

Every day, over 100,000 flights cross the sky. Here's how much pollution all of us create, how planes compare to cars, ships, cows and even volcanoes, and what we can actually do about it.

Part 1: How much pollution do we cause?

When people talk about "pollution" and climate change, they usually mean greenhouse gases. These gases trap heat in the atmosphere. The big three are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and nitrous oxide (N₂O).

In 2024, humans released about 53.2 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases (measured as "CO₂-equivalent," which converts every gas into the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming). That works out to roughly 146 million tonnes every single day.

What the world's pollution is made of

  • 74.5% CO₂ from fossil fuels
  • 17.9% Methane (CH₄)
  • 4.8% Nitrous oxide (N₂O)
  • 2.8% F-gases (from fridges, ACs, industry)

Share of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2024. Source: EDGAR (European Commission).

Pollution from one everyday thing, per day

Big numbers are hard to picture, so here's what one of each thing releases in a day:

🚗 One car
~12.6 kg CO₂
🐄 One dairy cow
~10 kg CO₂e
🧍 One person breathing
~1 kg CO₂

Car: EPA says a typical passenger car emits about 4.6 tonnes of CO₂ a year, or about 404 grams per mile. Cow: about 138 kg of methane a year from digestion, equal to about 3.7 tonnes of CO₂e. A beef cow emits roughly half that. Per-day numbers are calculated from yearly figures.

Wait, does breathing count as pollution? Not really. The carbon you breathe out came from food, and plants pulled that carbon out of the air recently. It's part of a natural loop. Burning gasoline or jet fuel is different: it releases carbon that was locked underground for millions of years.

Part 2: How do planes compare?

~103,000flights a day worldwide (early Nov 2026)
~26 tCO₂ per flight, on average*
~2,000cars' daily driving = one average flight*

On an average day in 2025, the world's airlines released about 2.7 million tonnes of CO₂. Their busiest day ever was July 18, 2025, when passenger jets alone released about 2.52 million tonnes, just above the previous record from August 2019.

Spread across roughly 103,000 flights a day, that's about 26 tonnes of CO₂ per flight, the same as about 2,000 cars driving for a whole day. Long-haul flights emit far more than this average, and short hops far less.

So are planes a big part of the problem?

By CO₂ alone, all of aviation (domestic and international flights) made up about 2.5% of the world's CO₂ in 2023. That sounds small, but there's a twist.

The hidden part: contrails

  • ~1/3 from CO₂
  • ~2/3 from contrails, NOx, water vapour and soot

Planes don't just release CO₂. High up, they also create contrails, the white lines you see in the sky. Some spread out into thin cirrus clouds that trap heat. Engines also release nitrogen oxides (NOx), which add more warming.

Scientists estimate these "non-CO₂" effects cause about two-thirds of aviation's warming. Counting everything, aviation is responsible for about 3.5% of all human-caused climate change. Planes release almost no methane or nitrous oxide, so their non-CO₂ impact comes from these other effects instead.

Myth check: volcanoes

Myth"One volcano eruption puts out more CO₂ than all humans."
FactHumans release as much CO₂ in 3 to 5 days as all the world's volcanoes do in a whole year.

All the world's volcanoes release roughly 0.13 to 0.44 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year. That means all the world's planes release about 4 times more CO₂ per day than every volcano on Earth combined.* Even huge eruptions like Mount St. Helens (1980) and Pinatubo (1991) matched human CO₂ output for only about nine hours.

Part 3: Is there anything we can do?

Yes. Some of it is up to airlines and engineers, and some of it is up to us.

What airlines and scientists are working on

🛫

More efficient planes

Newer jets and better operations cut aviation's carbon per tonne carried by about 20% between 2018 and 2025. The catch: more flights mean total emissions still went up.

🌿

Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF)

Fuel made from waste, plants or captured carbon. It's growing slowly: in 2025, SAF and offsets only cut about 1% of airline CO₂.

☁️

Avoiding contrails

Since contrails cause so much warming, researchers are testing small route and altitude changes to steer planes around the air where long-lasting contrails form.

⚡

Electric and hydrogen planes

Still early, and mostly for small, short-range aircraft for now, but they could cut emissions a lot in the future.

What you can do

🚆

Take the train for short trips

For short distances, trains and buses usually release much less per person than flying.

🎯

Fly nonstop

Takeoff and climbing burn a lot of fuel, so one direct flight is usually better than two connecting ones.

💺

Fly economy, pack light

More passengers share the fuel in economy, and a lighter plane burns less.

🚲

Cut everyday emissions

Cars are a much bigger part of most people's footprint than flights. Walking, biking, carpooling and transit all add up.

The bottom line

Planes are a real but fairly small slice of the world's pollution: about 2.5% of CO₂, and around 3.5% of human-caused warming once contrails are counted. Cars, power plants and industry are much bigger. But flying is one of the fastest-growing sources, and one long flight can outweigh months of driving for a single person. The good news is that cleaner fuels, smarter routes and better planes are all on the way, and small choices add up too.
