How Much Do Planes Really Pollute?

Where the world's greenhouse gases come from, how much the roughly 100,000 flights a day add to that, and what could bring it down.

November 2026

Greenhouse gases are gases that trap heat in the atmosphere and warm the planet. The main one is carbon dioxide (CO₂), released whenever coal, oil or gas is burned. The others are methane, nitrous oxide and a group of industrial "F-gases." To add them all together, scientists convert each gas into the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming. This is called CO₂-equivalent, or CO₂e.

In 2024, humans released about 53 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases, not counting forests and land use.1 That's roughly 146 million tonnes every day. About three-quarters of it was CO₂ from fossil fuels, and most of the rest was methane.1

Where our emissions come from

The most widely used breakdown comes from the UN's climate science panel, the IPCC. It sorts every human source into five sectors. This count includes cutting down forests, which pushes the total up to about 59 billion tonnes for 2019.2

Energy supply 34%
Power plants burning coal and gas to make electricity and heat, plus the oil, gas and coal industry itself, including gas that leaks out during drilling and transport.
Industry 24%
Factories making steel, cement, chemicals and other materials. Some comes from fuel burned on site, and some from chemical reactions, like heating limestone to make cement.
Farming, forests and land use 22%
Cutting down forests, methane from cows and other livestock, fertilizer, and flooded rice fields.
Transport 15%
The fuel burned by cars, trucks, buses, planes, ships and trains.
Buildings 6%
Gas and oil burned for heating and cooking in homes, offices and stores. The electricity those buildings use is counted under energy supply, which is why this share looks small.

Planes fall under transport. Within that sector, road vehicles are by far the biggest part. Aviation is a distant second, just ahead of shipping.3

Put those two charts together and aviation comes out at about 1.8% of all greenhouse gas emissions (12% of transport's 15%). Counting CO₂ alone, it's about 2.6% of the CO₂ from energy: 983 million tonnes out of 37.8 billion.4,5

Every number in one place

Planes next to everything else

Every day, the world's airlines burn enough fuel to release about 2.7 million tonnes of CO₂, almost exactly the same as all the world's ships. Spread across about 106,000 commercial flights a day,9 that's about 25 tonnes per flight. That's the same as about 2,000 cars each driving for a day. It's for the whole plane, not one passenger, and long flights release far more than short ones.

Volcanoes are often blamed for climate change, but planes alone release nearly four times as much CO₂ as every volcano on Earth combined. Humans release as much CO₂ from energy in under three days as all volcanoes do in a year.7 Even the giant eruptions of Mount St. Helens in 1980 and Pinatubo in 1991 matched human CO₂ output for only about nine hours each.8

The part CO₂ numbers miss

Planes warm the planet more than their CO₂ alone suggests. At cruising height, they leave contrails, the white lines across the sky. Some spread into thin clouds that trap heat. Jet engines also release nitrogen oxides, which add more warming. The most complete study so far found that these extra effects cause about two-thirds of aviation's warming.11

Counting everything, the same study estimated that flying causes about 3.5% of human-caused warming.11,12 So all three ways of measuring point the same way: aviation is about 1.8% of greenhouse gases, about 2.6% of energy CO₂, and about 3.5% of warming. It's a real share, but a small one next to power plants, industry, farming and road traffic. The concern is the direction. Planes keep getting more efficient, but the number of flights grows faster, so total emissions keep rising.4

What can be done

Because aviation is a small part of a much bigger picture, the largest cuts in emissions will come from the biggest sectors: cleaner electricity, cleaner industry, less deforestation and cleaner cars. Aviation still has to do its share, and it has three main options. The first is more efficient planes and routes, which already cut fuel per trip but haven't kept up with growth. The second is sustainable aviation fuel, made from waste, plants or captured carbon. It's the industry's main hope, but in 2025 it cut only about 1% of airline CO₂, even when combined with carbon offsets.4 The third is avoiding contrails. Long-lasting contrails only form in certain cold, humid air, so small changes in altitude could prevent many of them, and researchers are studying how to do that safely.

For travellers, the biggest choice is how often to fly. For short trips, a train or bus usually releases much less per person. When flying does make sense, a nonstop flight avoids a second takeoff and climb, the most fuel-hungry part of any trip, and economy seats spread each flight's emissions across more people.
